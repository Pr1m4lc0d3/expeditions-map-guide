The land

Using real-world terrain

Importing real elevation data, and the surprising amount of design work it does for you.

This page is draft. It may be incomplete or unverified. Anything marked unverified has not been proven in a running game.

You do not have to invent your landscape. Public survey data gives you the height of real ground, and a real place has already solved problems that are hard to solve by hand: where the valleys drain, how steep the slopes sit, and where a road can climb a hillside.

This page covers where that data comes from, what it does and does not contain, and the checks that catch the mistakes it makes easy.

Where the data comes from#

source coverage resolution notes
USGS 3DEP United States 1 m bare-earth lidar, no API key. Best by far
global terrain tiles (AWS terrarium set) worldwide roughly 10 to 30 m too coarse for roads to show up

measured

Bare-earth means the survey has had trees and buildings removed, leaving the ground itself. At 1 m that is finer than the map’s own height grid, which on a full-size map is 0.5 m per sample in the built terrain and 2 m per sample in the import file.

The 3DEP service refuses very large requests, so a full map is fetched in pieces of about 2000 pixels each and stitched. measured

Fetching it with the toolkit#

fetch_terrain.py fetches 3DEP for a point, crops it to the map’s size and writes the import file at the size the editor expects. It also reports the Max height to use and the height step that results.

python fetch_terrain.py <lat> <lon> --span 2016 --blocks 84
python fetch_terrain.py <lat> <lon> --span 2016 --blocks 84 --level level_<name> --apply

Then open the map in the editor and use Copy Source Heightmap to import it. Read how terrain works first: the import replaces the whole terrain, and a baked terrain file left from a copied map will hide the result.

Outside the United States the toolkit’s expeditions/dem.py uses the global tiles instead. That route has not been tested on a map in this guide.

Precision follows real elevation#

The height format’s smallest step grows with height above zero, not with the map’s relief. A map whose ground sits at 1,600 to 1,977 m above sea level would be stored with far coarser steps than a valley near sea level if those heights were kept as they are. Lower the floor so the lowest point of the map sits near zero. That is what buys precision. measured

Real terrain already contains its roads#

A road is a physical thing: a cut-and-fill bench in the hillside. Bare-earth lidar measures the ground, so it measures the road as part of it.

That means the road’s design comes with the data. Its gradient, where the switchbacks go and how deep it cuts were solved on the ground by whoever built it. You do not need to invent an alignment for a real place.

It was checked on the Moki Dugway in Utah, by looking only at the elevation for flat ribbons hemmed in by steep ground (slope under 10 degrees, with more than 20 m of relief within 41 m): measured

found from elevation alone documented
about 4,065 m of road 4,800 m (3 miles)
benches spanning 311 m of climb 366 m (1,200 ft)

Both within about 15%.

The terrain contains the road, but cannot say which bench it is

The same test on a canyon road in Canyonlands, Utah, reported 23,518 m of road against 7,193 m actually mapped, three times too much. Canyonlands is benches: mesa tops and rim ledges are natural flat ground between cliffs, and they pass the test perfectly. measured

On benched or mesa country, take the route from mapped road data and use the elevation only for height. Detecting benches is a way to find candidates, not a way to route.

Road centre lines as lines on a map are available from OpenStreetMap or the US National Map. The toolkit’s fetch_trails.py pulls tracks from OpenStreetMap and lays them as overlays, with Flatten on and ApplyOffset off. The line comes from the mapping, and the height comes from the terrain, because an overlay has no height of its own.

python fetch_trails.py level_<name> <lat> <lon> --span 2016
python fetch_trails.py level_<name> <lat> <lon> --span 2016 --apply

Getting the orientation right#

It is easy to import a real place mirror-imaged, and hard to notice. A valley looks like a valley either way round. What gives it away is that two layers built from the same place stop agreeing: the roads no longer sit on the benches in the ground.

That exact failure happened. The terrain fetch skipped the step that turns a north-up survey image into the map’s frame, while the road fetch applied it, so terrain and roads were mirror images of each other. Every check on the map passed, because each check looked at one layer on its own. A check of one layer cannot see a disagreement between layers. measured

The check that caught it scores how many road points land on benches found in the terrain:

reading road points on a bench
chance 14.7%
broken map, as built 10.6%, worse than chance
broken map, flipped east to west 53.5%
after the fix, as built 53.7%

measured

So: after importing a real place, overlay the roads on the terrain and confirm they agree. In the toolkit:

python check_new_map.py <name> --relief <metres>

It takes the map name without the level_ prefix and the relief reported by the fetch.

Do not trust a compass guess

Work out which way round a map is from evidence, such as where a change you made shows up in the files, never from an assumption like “west is on the left”. On one map, the side that had just been edited was at the high-x end, the opposite of the obvious guess. measured

Water is never in the elevation data#

The survey gives you the valley, the banks and the full cross-section. It does not give you a riverbed or a lake bed.

  • USGS hydro-flattening levels every body of water of 0.8 ha (2 acres) or more to a flat, level surface, by specification.
  • The infrared lidar used does not see through water anyway.

On the Buffalo River in Arkansas, the valley came through 142 m deep with both bluffs. The water surface varied by 0.092 m across a 35 x 36 m patch: a plane, not a channel. measured

For the water’s course, the US National Hydrography Dataset (NHD) gives stream centre lines, flow direction, connections and lake outlines, which is what a river object needs. It gives no depth.

For the bed, sources exist but cover only some waters:

  • USGS inland bathymetry, for selected lakes, rivers and reservoirs
  • existing combined land-and-water surveys for a few large lakes, including Lake Powell, Lake Tahoe and Crater Lake
  • US Army Corps of Engineers sonar surveys
  • state natural resource departments, several of which publish lake depth data
  • for a reservoir, maps made before the dam. The drowned valley floor is the bed

None of these has been imported into a map in this guide yet. See rivers, lakes and waterfalls for building the water itself.

Matching the colour of a real place#

Satellite imagery providers disagree with each other, a lot, about colour. Over one desert map, four sources gave: measured

source hue reads as
Google satellite 11 to 19 dusty pink
ground photographs (Street View) 60 to 75 tan
USGS NAIP aerial, 1 m 72 to 78 warm tan
Esri World Imagery about 90 olive tan

The ground was painted to match the first source and came out about 60 degrees of hue too pink. Two independent sources, the aerial survey and the ground photographs, agreed with each other; the provider the map was matched to was the odd one out.

One imagery source is not a reference. Compare several from separate pipelines, and include photographs taken on the ground.

Lightness and saturation were already right on that map; only the hue was off. The corrected textures were confirmed in the game files but have not yet been checked by eye in the editor. More on this in colour and textures.

  • No bed data has been imported for a lake or river yet.
