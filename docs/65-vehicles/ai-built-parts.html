Building parts with AI

Bumpers, racks, roof-top tents, fuel cans, recovery boards, axles and winches made with an AI assistant, an image model and Tripo or Meshy. The order that works, and the mistakes that each cost a day or a few hundred credits.

This page is draft. It may be incomplete or unverified. Anything marked unverified has not been proven in a running game.

Building parts with AI#

An AI coding assistant, an image model and a 3D generator such as Tripo or Meshy can between them make most of what a truck carries: bumpers, roof racks, roll cages, tube fenders, a roof-top tent, fuel cans, a parts case, recovery boards, a winch, a Dana axle housing. None of them gets it right by being asked nicely. What follows is the order that works, learned one rejected part at a time.

There are two ways to make a part, and choosing the wrong one is the most expensive mistake:

The part is Make it Because
tube and plate: a rack, a cage, a bumper frame, sliders, tube fenders in code, from measured centre lines tube must be round, straight, welded and exactly where the body is. A generator gives you a lumpy approximation of all three
a manufactured object: a case, a fuel can, a tent, a winch, an axle housing, recovery boards with a 3D generator, from a real photo the shape is the whole point, and nobody wants to model a can’s ribs by hand

Tube parts in code covers the first. This page covers the second, then fitting either kind to the truck.

1. Start from a real object#

Every good generated part on this project started from a photo of a real one: a maker’s product shot, an install drawing, or the owner’s own truck. Every part made from a description (“a rugged parts case”, “a period-style fuel can”) came back looking invented, because it was.

  • Find the actual product. Pull several views if the maker shows them.
  • If the maker publishes install drawings, use them. One roof rack was rebuilt from the maker’s own install PDF after a “looks like a rack” version was rejected, and the drawings gave every part.
  • Show the photos to whoever the part is for before going further. A set of downloaded photos is cheap to reject; a set of models is not.

2. Make a clean concept from the photo#

Generators build from one or a few images, and they build everything in the image: the background, the reflection on the paint, the strap hanging off the side, the logo.

  • Cut the object out of the photo with a hand-checked mask. Look at the cut-out at full size, not as a thumbnail. A coloured fringe round the edge becomes a coloured rim on the model. One batch of eight concepts went to the generator with fringes left in; seven of the eight models were unusable.
  • Paint out every logo and maker’s lettering. A generated model reproduces them, and a published mod must not carry someone else’s brand.
  • Re-light shiny objects with soft, even light first. Reflections turn into false seams: on one generated car body the “panel gaps” sat exactly on the reflection bands of the photos.
  • A part cut out of a concept sheet (one bumper from a drawing of the whole truck) generates far better than a part described in words. Four hand-built versions of one bumper were rejected; the first generation from a cut-out of the drawing got the design right.
  • Hidden sides get the fewest features that make sense. Every door, step or window you invent on a side nobody drew is a guess someone has to catch.
  • Get an OK on the concept before spending credits on it.

3. Generate#

For Tripo, the settings that mattered:

  • Turn off any polygon reduction in the generator. A vehicle generated at a 60,000-polygon cap came out with 37 % open edges and 2,199 loose fragments; the same input at 2,000,000 polygons and 4K texture came out as one piece with 20 open edges. Reduce it yourself afterwards.
  • For a whole vehicle, use the multi-view mode (front, back, left, right), with the right view made by mirroring the left and flipping its badges back.
  • One generation per clean concept. A retry reuses the same input, so a dirty input wastes every retry.

4. Clean the model, do not rebuild it#

The generated model is the asset. Fix its faults; do not replace it with something you modelled because it was easier to control. A “cleaned” truck body rebuilt from boxes measured off the generated one lost everything that made it look right.

  • Prove a fault before fixing it: mark it in red on a render and look.
  • Common faults: photo background baked into the model, invented backing plates, mask leftovers, a hoop generated as a half shell.
  • Where a fault is fused into a real part, rebuild that part only, on a centre line measured from the generated mesh.
  • Cut exactly. Split the mesh on the cut line first, then delete. Deleting faces by their centre point and filling the hole leaves stepped, spiky edges.
  • Weld UV seams before cutting holes in a generated mesh.

5. Fit it to the truck#

  • Scale with one uniform scale to a measured feature (the real product’s length, a wheelbase, the hub centres). A part stretched to fit looks wrong even when every number says it fits.
  • Use the part you were given. If someone hands you a model, fit that model. Replacing it with your own build because the first fit was awkward throws away their work.
  • Rest it on what it sits on. A can on a rack is levelled by its own top and dropped onto the bars, not levelled by its base: one generated can had a base plate skewed 2.4 degrees, so every base-levelled copy leaned.
  • Render it on the truck, with back-face culling on, from the angle the player sees, on both sides.
  • Fit the gear to the design, not the design to the gear. When a real bumper’s can holders were sized for real cans, the fix for a stock can that was too big was a new can, not a new bumper.
  • Look at what the model already has before building what seems to be missing. On one ported truck, five rebuilds of a “missing” door jamb were wasted: the original model had the whole jamb, and our own cutting script was removing it. Render the untouched original against a magenta background (holes show as magenta) and compare it with your patched version first.
  • Measure from orthographic views, not perspective screenshots. A colour-coded side render with the see-through pixels counted found a gap that a dozen perspective looks had argued about. In a player’s screenshot, work out which side the camera is on before reading left and right.

Keep the model intact through conversion#

A generated model can look perfect as it came from the generator and wrong in game, because of what happened to it on the way. Render the raw generated file and your converted file side by side before installing anything. On one set of generated wheel rims that comparison was the whole diagnosis.

  • Keep its own shading normals. Merging vertices, recomputing normals or re-smoothing a generated mesh melted a rim’s bolts into lumps and crumpled its centre cap. Move the model with object transforms, keep the generator’s UVs, normals and normal map, and do not reduce it if it fits the budget.
  • Check which side the game draws. The same rims showed a plain disc in game for a day: the game was drawing their back. Render the converted file from the side the game shows and compare it with a screenshot before blaming the textures.
  • Paint per texel, and give solid black its own material. Recolouring a generated texture by rough regions bleeds colour across edges. Rasterise every triangle into a map of which face each texel belongs to, colour by face, and put large flat black areas on a second material with a small solid-black texture: at a distance the game blends texture levels, and a black ring sharing a texture with an olive face came out smeared olive.
  • Paint out invented lettering. A generator will print convincing nonsense text on a tent cover or a case. Paint it out of the texture (and the normal map) before the part goes anywhere.

6. Into the game#

Every mesh the game loads has to pass through the game’s own converter, and the game’s limits apply to generated parts like any other:

  • 65,534 triangles per mesh. Two generated parts in one add-on mesh almost always need reducing.
  • Normal maps from glTF are green-up; the game reads green-down. Flip the green channel when you convert them.
  • Each add-on mesh must carry its material, or the game draws nothing and says nothing.
  • An add-on needs a slot on the truck and, if it bolts to another add-on, a requirement on that add-on. That is its own page: Add-on slots, dependencies and gear.

What has been checked#

Everything above comes from parts that were built, installed and looked at in game, and from the mistakes along the way. The credit and polygon numbers are from those runs. The rim lessons were confirmed in game once the rims were turned and painted per texel. What is not here yet: a side-by-side of Tripo against Meshy on the same input, and rigging a generated part to move (a winch drum, a door).
